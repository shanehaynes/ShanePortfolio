Reasons made under pressure serve the pressure

Kierkegaard wrote a small book about a lily and a bird. It is about worry, and how to set it down. I keep it on the shelf with four others I have gone back to for the same reason. I want an axiom to act from, and I want it before I need it.

The obvious objection is that reasons come from living and not from reading, and it is mostly right. But living arrives at speed, and it does not wait while I work out what I believe.

Owning a thing means staying for the answer

I am a synthesizer rather than a specialist. What holds my attention is the full arc of a problem: finding it, winning the argument for it, building the thing, and being responsible for what happens next.

My work has been in teaching systems to talk to each other, then teaching them to think, and lastly teaching the people who would own them next. I build guided by a philosophy rather than for building's sake, and I treat what I ship as a hypothesis the world gets to answer.

No cathedral more sublime, no crucible more stern

Cooper broke trail up the whole couloir so the rest of us would not have to. We did not talk about it at the top. Most of what I know about the people I adventure with, I learned on mornings like that one, and none of it was said out loud.

The climbs are committing and the ski lines are steep. I pick them that way. Most of my time away from a laptop is spent under the sun, even if transitively by way of thick cloud or fog, and I keep going back to places that would not notice if I stopped.

Writing is thinking tested publicly

An argument that feels whole in my head usually has a hole in the third paragraph, and I only find it by writing the third paragraph. That much is selfish.

The rest is not. I write about philosophy, technology, and the strange shapes social life takes, and an idea kept to yourself ossifies. Published, other people push on it, and it either grows or withers. Both of those are worth finding out.
